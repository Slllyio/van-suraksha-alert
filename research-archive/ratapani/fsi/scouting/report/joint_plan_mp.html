Fire lines and control burning, together

A plan for the forests of Madhya Pradesh, built from nine seasons of satellite burn scars

Terrain-following fire lines on nalas, crests, tracks and forest edges; late-February block burning of the ground that burns every year; how the two fit, where to do each, in which order, and how much forest it keeps out of the fire.

All MP, 82 forest divisions with scar data, seasons 2017-2025 (153,817 forest fires, 30 m burn outlines). Every saving quoted is measured on the held-out seasons 2022-25 unless marked otherwise.

20 October 2026. Research thread, FSI scouting. Source note: ratapani/fsi/scouting/joint_plan.md; data and code code/joint_plan/.

Summary

The two tools sit on the same ground. Of the 4,986 km of practical fire lines, 97% (4,840 km) run inside the 3,867 repeat-burn patches that are also the places to control-burn. They are not two programmes to add up; they are one programme with two uses for the same cleared lines: as the boundaries of the February burn blocks where a patch is burnt, and as fire breaks where it is not.

The rule. Burn every repeat patch that can be burnt, between 15 and 28 February, every year, block by block inside its practical lines. Where a patch cannot be burnt (steep ground, mahua and tendu collection, wildlife, no crew), keep the same lines as fire breaks. Put fire watchers on the ranked squares from 8 March. Lines are cleared in December-January, before the burns.

What it saves. Forest that is not burnt at all tops out at about 45 thousand ha a year (6% of MP's 719 thousand ha of burnt forest), and most of that is reached by the first programme: burn the first 100 thousand ha of patches (465 patches in 55 divisions) and keep breaks through the rest, for 43 thousand ha not burnt plus 38 thousand ha of April wildfire turned into cool February burns: 81 thousand ha a year, 11% of MP's burnt forest, for about Rs 2.3 crore a year (lines at the CAMPA rate, burning at crew wages). Burning all 419 thousand ha of patches touches 153 thousand ha a year (21%) for Rs 8 crore.

Checked since. Displacement to neighbouring ground is small and uncertain; the burnt blocks can be audited from the next satellite pass outside the sal belt; every patch carries an escape flag from its own record; the patch map must be rebuilt yearly (section 7).

Why the ceiling. The repeat patches carry 20% of MP's burnt forest and start 21% of its fires over 200 ha; the other 80% burns on ground that burns once in 28 years, where neither a line nor a burn can be placed in advance. That ground is the live-alert and watcher job, not a prevention job.

2. Tool one: practical fire lines along the terrain

A fire line is a cleared strip a fire cannot cross without help. From MP's own fires (research/fire-continuity), a fire's chance of stopping at a road is 3.3x, at the forest edge 2.1x, at cropland 1.8x the chance in open forest; a built line in the compartment record holds 70% of the time (fire_lines_v2.md, 2022-25). The lines here are drawn for the repeat patches only, as cuts that split a patch into compartments so that a fire starting in one does not take the whole patch, and each cut is the cheapest path across the compartment on a cost surface that prefers existing roads and tracks, nala lines, the forest edge and ridge crests, and doubles the cost of running up a 20% grade. Of the 4,986 km, 21% lies on nala lines, 15% on ridge crests, 3% on the forest edge, 1% on roads or tracks; 59% still crosses forest interior, along contours, because inside these patches that is all there is (the DEM is 150 m; crews should snap each stretch to the nearest nala or crest it cannot see). 10% of the length is steeper than 15%.

3. Tool two: control burning the repeat patches in late February

Burnt ground cannot burn again until the litter is back. Measured from the VIIRS heat points over 82 divisions (a second burn leaves no second scar, so heat is the only way to see it), ground burnt two to four weeks earlier gets 0.42x the fire of unburnt forest in the same 5 km square and week, four to six weeks 0.22x, and about 0.20x from then to the end of the season; deep inside a block (180 m from any unburnt cell) protection is near total after four weeks. A February, March or April burn protects alike. The protection is one season: ground burnt last season gets 1.8x more fire this season, not less, because one monsoon puts the litter back. At the landscape scale, each forest ha burnt between 15 February and 15 March takes 0.19 ha (0.12-0.26) off the rest of the season in the same square, after square and division-season fixed effects; without them the sign flips, which is why plain before/after comparisons mislead.

3.1 Only ground that burns anyway is worth burning

A control burn on ordinary forest (3.6% chance of burning) prevents 0.03 ha of wildfire per ha burnt and the fuel is back next year. On a repeat patch (37% a season held out, 53-63% over nine seasons) it prevents or converts 0.3-0.6 ha per ha burnt, plus 10-13 forest ha outside the patch per 100 ha burnt, because the fires that start on these patches spread out of them. The programme is the patches, nothing else. Burning strips of ordinary forest "to reduce fuel load" is not supported by the record.

4. Putting the two together

4.1 They compete for the same ground

Joining the two maps: 7,347 of the 7,703 practical lines (4,840 of 4,986 km, 97%) run inside a repeat patch, and 736 of the 3,867 patches carry lines. A patch that is burnt in February does not need its lines as fire breaks that season: the patch cannot carry a March fire. So the saving of a line and the saving of a burn on the same patch cannot be added. Under the full burning programme the lines' own held-out saving inside burnt patches (25 thousand ha a year) is forgone, and only 146 km of lines remain as breaks.

4.2 But a burn needs the lines

A block burn is only safe inside a boundary. The practical lines are exactly that: they already follow the nalas, crests, tracks and edges inside the patch, they split it into blocks of a size a crew can burn in a day, and they are cleared in December-January, before the litter peak. So in the combined plan every line inside a patch to be burnt becomes a burn-block boundary, and the patch edge gets a boundary where it has no natural barrier ([inference] 60% of the perimeter; the rest is a nala, road, crop or forest edge). The line programme and the burn programme share one set of cleared lines.

The coordination rule, patch by patch
  1. Can the patch be burnt? (crew available 15-28 Feb; not too steep for a crew to hold; no mahua or tendu collection in the window; no wildlife objection.) The record cannot tell this; the range officer can.
  2. If yes: burn it, every year, 15-28 February, one block a day, from the downwind block inward, inside its practical lines; clear the lines and the edge boundary first. Count: the patch's own hectares turned into a cool burn, plus 10-13 forest ha stopped outside per 100 ha, every year.
  3. If no: keep the practical lines as fire breaks (re-cleared every January). Count: the held-out line saving, 6-12 forest ha per km per year.
  4. Never burn ground outside the patches, and never put a line where no patch is: both are worth 0.03 ha per ha and nothing next year.
  5. From 8 March, fire watchers on the ranked squares (section 5.3): the burnt patches no longer need them; the once-in-28-years ground does.

5. How much forest it saves

5.1 Two kinds of saving

The honest answer has two parts, and they should be kept apart.

  • Forest not burnt at all: the hectares outside a burnt patch that fires starting inside it would have reached, and the hectares a fire break holds back. This is what "area saved" usually means. It tops out at about 45 thousand ha a year (6% of MP's burnt forest) whichever mix of the two tools is used, because both act on the same 419 thousand ha of patches and the fires that leave them.
  • Forest burnt cool in February instead of hot in late March: the patch's own hectares. This is not forest saved from fire; it is the same ground burnt on purpose, six weeks earlier, 30% cooler, in fires a third the size, with crews standing at the line. Whether that is worth having depends on what a February burn does to regeneration and wildlife compared with the April wildfire the ground gets now; the satellite record cannot say. Up to 108 thousand ha a year (15% of MP's burnt forest) can be moved this way.

Together, the full programme touches 153 thousand ha a year, 21% of MP's burnt forest; the first programme (100 thousand ha burnt plus breaks elsewhere) touches 81 thousand ha (11%), of which 43 thousand ha is forest that does not burn.

5.2 Cost per hectare

Lines alone are the cheapest way to keep a hectare out of the fire: Rs 267 per ha not burnt at the full 4,986 km (Rs 0.68 crore a year, 25 thousand ha). Adding the burning of the first 25 thousand ha raises the forest not burnt to 36 thousand ha at Rs 301 per ha; the first 100 thousand ha, 43 thousand ha at Rs 540 per ha not burnt, or Rs 288 per ha touched; the full programme Rs 1,763 per ha not burnt, Rs 518 per ha touched. Burning is the expensive way to keep forest out of the fire and the only way to change what happens to the patches themselves.

[inference] Crews: at 15 ha per crew-day, the first programme is 6,663 crew-days in a 12-working-day window, about 555 crews of five (2,776 people) statewide for the fortnight; the full programme 27,935 crew-days, 2,328 crews (11,639 people). MP already fields about 4,730 fire watchers for the season, so the first programme is within the existing seasonal workforce if the watchers start after the burns.

6.1 Inside one patch

  1. Walk the practical lines with the map (GeoJSON in the files below); snap each stretch to the nearest nala, crest or track the 150 m DEM did not see; clear the edge boundary where the patch has no natural barrier.
  2. Number the blocks; the day's block is the one downwind; light in the afternoon from the downwind line back into the wind, the crew on the line.
  3. Do not leave unburnt pockets: the clock's 2-4 week ratio of 0.42 (not 0.05) is partly pockets and edges that carry a later fire.
  4. Check the block in the next clear satellite pass (the live-alert system draws the scar); re-burn pockets before 15 March.

6.2 What makes a patch a "cannot burn"

Steep ground (the line table gives the km steeper than 15% per line), a village or mahua and tendu collection inside the window, a tiger reserve's own rules, or simply no crew that fortnight. These patches keep their lines as breaks and move to the watcher list. The plan does not lose much: a lined patch saves 6-12 ha per km, a burnt one 10-13 outside ha per 100 ha plus its own conversion, so the forest-not-burnt total barely moves (section 5.1) when a patch switches from burn to lines.

8. What this plan cannot promise

  • The 6% ceiling is real. 80% of MP's burnt forest is on ground that does not burn every year. No line or planned burn can be placed on it in advance. That burning is reduced only by catching fires early (live alerts, 2-day list, watchers), which this plan funds by freeing the watchers from the burnt patches.
  • The conversion is not a saving. The 108 thousand ha turned into February burns are still burnt. The record shows those burns are 30% cooler and a third the size; it shows nothing about teak regeneration, mahua, tendu or ground-nesting wildlife. That is a field question, outside this analysis.
  • Displacement. Tested to 15 km (section 7.1): small and uncertain for February burns, between zero and the full own-square saving. Beyond 15 km, and for the first year of a real programme, unmeasured; the first-year scar checks settle it.
  • Costs are partly inferred. The line rate is the CAMPA rate; the burning rate and the edge-boundary share are inferences marked as such. The per-hectare figures change with them, the ranking of patches and divisions does not.
  • Lines are held out, the set is not. The held-out saving per km comes from lines chosen on 2017-21 and scored on 2022-25; the final line set was drawn on all nine seasons and scaled to the held-out level. Patches are ranked on 2017-21 and scored on 2022-25 throughout.
  • Per-division protection varies (re-burn ratios 0.05-0.71 across divisions; the sal belt sheds litter late). The first-year scar checks will show where the clock runs differently.

Appendix D. Method in brief

  • Lattice. 60 m cells (EPSG:32643), forest = WorldCover tree and shrub; each scar outline rasterised per fire with its start cells and day of burn.
  • Repeat patches. Cells burnt in 3+ of the 5 seasons 2017-21 (tr3), 8-connected, at least 10 forest ha; all5 = 5+ of 9 seasons. Patch value: fires 2022-25 whose majority start cell is in the patch; their forest ha inside the patch (converted) and outside it (stopped), times 0.70 protection; ranked on benefit per 100 ha.
  • Lines. Candidate cuts across each compartment as least-cost paths (roads 0.20, nalas 0.30, forest edge 0.40, open 0.50, crests 0.50, forest 1.00, grade penalty); greedy choice on the drop-best-year objective; scoring: a fire spreads again inside its own outline from its start, each line crossing stops it with chance 0.70; saved = forest ha of the outline beyond the line. Held out: chosen 2017-21, scored 2022-25.
  • Fuel clock. VIIRS heat points on burnt cells relative to unburnt forest in the same division, season, 5 km square and week (indirect standardisation), bootstrap over division-seasons; landscape leverage by two-way fixed-effects regression (square, division x season).
  • Joining. Each practical line is assigned to the repeat patch it mostly runs through (within 150 m). A burnt patch's lines count as block boundaries (cost only); an unburnt patch's lines count at their held-out saving. Programme sizes take patches in rank order of burn benefit; everything else keeps its breaks.
