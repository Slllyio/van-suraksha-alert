SETTING THE FRAME
Why a Cross-Continental Lens
Fire behaves differently on every continent — but the technology curve is converging fast.
🇨🇦
Canada — boreal crown fire regime
Record 2023 season (~18.5M ha burned); the Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System and FireSmart community program are the global reference points.
🇦🇺
Australia — eucalypt-driven extreme fire
Black Summer 2019–20 (~24M ha burned) reshaped national fire science; AFAC and CSIRO now run one of the world's fastest research-to-operations pipelines.
🇮🇳
India — monsoon-bound dry-season fires, and homegrown innovation
A short, intense Nov–Jun fire window; alongside FSI's satellite alerting, this session draws on case studies from Odisha, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Tamil Nadu, Nagaland and Maharashtra's Melghat shared during this training programme.
The physics of fire is universal — the tools to fight it are converging.

Why the Traditional Toolkit Falls Short
The Traditional Model
•  Fixed watchtowers with limited, weather-dependent line of sight
•  Foot and vehicle patrolling of fire lines
•  Verbal / wireless reporting up the Beat–Range–Division chain
•  Reactive suppression once smoke is visually confirmed
•  Manual, delayed burnt-area assessment after the season

•  Detection lag of hours in remote or hilly terrain

•  Data siloed between HQ, Division and Range

🗺️
A 26-module digital command layer

Nagaland's LoRaWAN Fire Detection Network
DETECT
PREDICT

PROPOSED DESIGN
📡
A purpose-built detection unit
A solar-powered pole (~3 m exposed, LiFePO4 battery backup) carries a 3-element flame sensor head, smoke/particulate intake, temperature sensors and a siren + beacon — firing on 2-out-of-3 sensor agreement to cut false alarms.
📶
Dual-path communication, built for remote terrain
LoRaWAN is the primary channel (long-range, low-power, star-of-stars gateway network); the unit automatically falls back to GSM/SMS if LoRaWAN coverage is unavailable — detection doesn't stop where connectivity does.
🖥️
One server stack behind every alert
A LoRaWAN network server and application server ingest alerts from every unit and gateway, run the alert engine, and expose the same data through a REST API — including a direct feed into FSI's Van Agni portal.
📱
Built for the field, not just the control room
An Android app gives field staff a live map of units and hotspots, real-time push alerts, incident navigation and status tracking — turning a sensor network into an actionable response tool.

MODULE 1 · DETECT
The Early-Detection Mechanism: Nagaland's Detection Unit
PROPOSED DESIGN
Three independent senses
An IR/UV flame head (3 elements, 120° apart), a smoke / particulate intake and a fast rate-of-rise temperature sensor, checked against a shielded ambient reference.
2-of-3 alarm logic
The unit fires only when two of the three agree — cutting false alarms from sun glint, dust or a single faulty sensor.
Warns locally, reports remotely
Siren and beacon alert the nearby community at once, while LoRaWAN (with GSM/SMS fallback) carries the alert to the server and the field app.
Built for unattended sites
Solar panel with LiFePO4 battery on a ~3 m pole, 0.65 m concrete embedment.

India's Own HPC Fire-Spread Forecast
DETECT
PREDICT
PREVENT
RESPOND
CASE STUDY
🖥️
Built on India's supercomputing backbone
C-DAC's PARAM Rudra (20 petaflop) supercomputer runs a coupled atmosphere–fire model (WRF-SFIRE), producing decision-grade 24–48 hour forest-fire spread forecasts — a capability India has not had at national scale before.
🏔️
Proven live in Sikkim, March 2024
HPC-based spread forecasts were used operationally for the first time in India: Forest Department teams intervened on the forecast's guidance and kept roughly 0.54 sq km and, days later, 3 sq km of forest from burning.
🤝
Going national
An FSI–C-DAC MoU is underway to build a National Wildfire Forecasting and Monitoring System — zoned for the Himalaya, Deccan Peninsula, Western Ghats, Northeast and rest of India — with proximity-based SMS alerts.
⏱️
Getting faster
Simulation time is already down to 17 minutes for a 24-hour forecast on 16 compute nodes, with multi-ignition-point modelling (200+ simultaneous points tested) under active development.

Mapping What Actually Burns
DETECT
PREDICT
PREVENT
RESPOND
CASE STUDY
🔬
Why surface fuel, specifically
Since 93% of India's fires start on the surface floor, FSI's national study measures dry leaf litter, duff, grass and downed wood directly through pan-India field sampling and oven-drying (3 hrs at ~50°C).
🗺️
A 272-stratum national model
Forest Type Map (17 classes) × Forest Canopy Map (4 density classes) × Slope (4 classes) are overlaid in GIS to produce 272 distinct strata, each assigned a fuel-load class from field-calibrated results.
📊
The national picture
Across India's mapped Recorded Forest Area, 2.08% carries Very High fuel load and 8.25% carries High fuel load (>40 g/ft²) — a ready-made targeting layer for fireline and fuel-treatment priority.
🛰️
Complementary to airborne LiDAR
Where airborne or UAV LiDAR captures 3D canopy structure, FSI's ground-truthed model captures the surface litter that actually ignites — the two techniques complement, not compete with, each other.

Turning Fuel Into Fortune: Chir Pirul
DETECT
PREDICT
PREVENT
RESPOND
CASE STUDY
🌲
The problem
Chir Pine covers 15.45% (3,890 sq km) of Uttarakhand's forests; its shed needles ('Pirul') blanket the forest floor as a highly combustible fuel carpet — the single biggest driver of the state's rapid-spread surface fires.
🔄
The four-step value chain
Van Panchayats & SHGs collect Pirul from the forest floor → mechanised/manual transport to processing units → pressing into pellets and briquettes → commercial sale to industry.
💰
Proven at scale
5,532 tonnes collected in 2025 alone (7,471 tonnes over 2022–24); ₹5.43 crore paid to collectors in 2025; pine-needle pellets (20.3 MJ/kg) are calorifically competitive with coal — ₹9/kg vs ₹38/kg for coal.
📈
Scaling further
48 of a planned 106 collection centres and 9 of 57 planned pellet/briquette units are operational, with 20 more units targeted over the next two years.

MODULE 3 · PREVENT
The Shitlakhet Model & the 'Golden Hour'
DETECT
PREDICT
PREVENT
RESPOND
CASE STUDY
🏔️
A century of community fire protection
Syahi Devi forest near Shitlakhet, Almora, became a reserved forest in 1877; organised community fire protection began there in 1890–91 — one of India's longest-running local fire-stewardship traditions.
🌳
From ashes to thriving forest
A landscape devastated by fire in 2012 was a mixed, regenerating forest by 2023 — a decade of sustained community vigilance, not new technology, made the difference.
⏱️
The Golden Hour doctrine
“The first 60 minutes determine survival.” Once a fire establishes in pine forest it is nearly impossible to stop without heavy machinery — so the model is built around decentralised, village-level rapid mobilisation via WhatsApp alerts, not waiting for departmental crews.
📋
A replicable blueprint
Jardhar Gaon (Tehri Garhwal) has since adopted the same principles — decentralise response to the village, institutionalise a cultural fire-awareness day ('Van/Aun Diwas'), and treat the Golden Hour as the organising principle.

In Central India, fire is a social phenomenon
In Sipna Wildlife Division, Melghat Tiger Reserve (Satpura dry teak forest, Maharashtra), practically every fire is lit by people — tendu and mahua collection, grazing, retaliation. So the 2020 plan put its weight on prevention through communities, not on suppression hardware.
💰
Phased, collective, paid in the village
Zero-fire rewards were announced before the season and paid by cheque to the village committee — rising from ₹10,000 to ₹50,000 for a buffer village and ₹20,000 to ₹1,00,000 for a core village. Phased, so a village that slips in April still has May to protect.
📉
The result, against a signed ground register
Fire incidents fell from 218 to 35 (−84%) and burnt area from 2,207 ha to 169 ha (−92%) in a single season. In the worst range, Hatru, 48 fires became 3 — and Bhutrum village went from 195 ha burnt to zero.
⚖️
The honest finding: it did not persist
Once the scheme lapsed, fires climbed back to 85 (2021) and 133 (2022), and 2025 was the worst season on record. Community incentives work — but only as a standing programme, not a one-season experiment.

MODULE 4 · RESPOND & LEARN  ·  CENTRAL INDIA
Melghat's Lesson: Perimeters, Not Pixels
DETECT
PREDICT
PREVENT
RESPOND
CASE STUDY
🗼
The watchtower beat the satellite by two hours
23 May 2020, compartment 551: a Forest Guard on watchtower duty reported smoke at 13:12 and the fire was controlled by 14:40 (7.8 ha). The NASA-FIRMS e-mail arrived at 15:08 — and no FSI alert was raised at all. The alert is the safety net, not the trigger.
🍂
In dry deciduous forest, dNBR reads leaf-fall
Run over a whole Feb–Jun season, Sentinel-2 dNBR flagged 44% of the division as burnt in both 2019 and 2020 — though the ground records differed by 92%. The index was calibrated for crown fire; Central India's is a litter fire. Imagery must bracket the fire date, not the season.
📐
A pixel is not a fire
A 375 m VIIRS detection arrives as a ~14 ha footprint, while the median Sipna fire was under 3 ha — pixel counts over-state area about four-fold. Divisional damage assessment needs GPS-walked or drone-mapped perimeters.
📒
Keep a season register — and feed it back
A compartment-wise, GPS-measured, ACF-verified register of every fire is what allows honest year-on-year comparison and reward adjudication — and it is exactly the ground truth that national burnt-area products need from divisions.
